# Writing techniques for the Golden Five revision

This is an editorial checklist for the next paper generation pass. It does not
provide scientific evidence. A technique earns its place only when it helps the
reader follow a supported argument.

| Technique | What the reader should see | Review question |
|---|---|---|
| Question and answer | The introduction states the research question; the abstract and conclusion give the same bounded answer. | Can a reader state the answer in one sentence? |
| Signposting | The introduction previews the argument's steps; a section opening says why that step is needed. | Can the reader predict why the next section follows? |
| Topic sentences | Each paragraph starts with its main point, then develops it with evidence or reasoning. | Does the first sentence tell the paragraph's job? |
| Claim, evidence, warrant | A result is followed by the specific observation, calculation, or citation and an explanation of how it supports the claim. | Is the inference explicit, including its limits? |
| Transitions | A short bridge names the logical relation between adjacent ideas: consequence, contrast, qualification, or remaining question. | Is there a reason for the order of paragraphs? |
| Definition before notation | Technical terms, symbols, and assumptions are introduced before they carry the argument. | Could a reader decode each equation on first encounter? |
| Equation interpretation | Prose before an equation states its purpose; prose after it explains the physical or mathematical consequence. | Does the equation do visible argumentative work? |
| Worked example | One concrete case traces the general method or theorem through its important steps. | Can the reader see how the general statement operates? |
| Alternatives and boundary cases | The paper compares plausible explanations or constructions and shows what the evidence excludes and leaves open. | Is the scope of the conclusion honest? |
| Explanatory figures | A diagram makes a relation, mechanism, or proof structure easier to grasp; its caption states what to notice. | Would removing the figure make comprehension harder? |
| Synthesis | The discussion reconnects results to the question and prior work; the conclusion states the achieved answer and open gap. | Does the ending follow from the body without adding a new claim? |

## Grounding

- [Purdue OWL, Paragraphs and Paragraphing](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/paragraphs_and_paragraphing/index.html) describes topic sentences, developed paragraphs, transitions, and signposts.
- [Purdue OWL, Revising for Cohesion](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/the_writing_process/proofreading/revising_for_cohesion.html) explains how topic sentences and repeated conceptual links help readers follow an argument.
- [Nature Portfolio, How to Write Your Paper](https://www.nature.com/nature-portfolio/for-authors/write) recommends a focused message, defined technical terms, and figures that support that message.
- [Nature Methods, So You're Writing a Paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/nmeth.4532) distinguishes the jobs of the introduction, results, and discussion and stresses an accurate main message in the abstract.
- [Nature Computational Science, On Writing Accessible Computational Science Papers](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43588-025-00847-0) recommends ending the introduction with a preview of approach, findings, and implications.

## Application to the five papers

The Warp paper is the clearest case for a question-and-answer spine: each
geometry, stress-energy, and feasibility section must show how it changes the
answer to whether a coherent physical framework permits effective faster than
light travel. A spacetime diagram should distinguish geometric shortcut from
local motion and identify the assumptions behind the inference. The other four
papers need the same structure adapted to their own research question, with
figures only where the saved evidence supports them.

This checklist is a review aid, not a license to invent results or turn an
unverified argument into a proof.
