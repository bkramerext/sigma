The Sharp Region Bound for Straight-Line Arrangements

Abstract
How many connected regions can finitely many full straight lines create in the Euclidean plane? A deductive argument gives the sharp maximum $M(n)=1+n(n+1)/2$ for every nonnegative integer $n$. The key observation is that a newly inserted line with $m$ distinct intersections with earlier lines creates exactly $m+1$ new regions. Convexity of the existing regions justifies this correspondence and makes the upper bound valid even when parallel, concurrent, or repeated lines occur. The explicit family $y=ix+i^2$, for $i=1,\ldots,n$, attains every incremental bound because its intersections with earlier lines are distinct. Matching the bound and construction proves the formula, including the empty arrangement. Small cases illustrate the count without replacing the general argument.

Problem framing
Let $n$ be a nonnegative integer, and consider $n$ full straight lines in the Euclidean plane. A region is a connected component of the complement of their union. Write $M(n)$ for the largest possible number of regions; parallel, concurrent, and repeated lines are permitted when establishing the universal bound.
Determining this maximum requires two separate arguments: an upper bound for every arrangement and a construction attaining that bound for each $n$. Counting a few examples cannot establish either requirement in general. The proof below relates the change in the region count to the distinct intersection points on each newly inserted line.

Methods and evidence
The analysis proceeds by inserting lines one at a time. It first identifies regions as nonempty intersections of open half-planes, then uses their convexity to establish an exact increment formula. Summing the resulting incremental bounds gives the universal upper bound.
Attainment is established separately by solving the pairwise intersection equations for an explicit family of lines. Distinct intersection coordinates verify that every insertion reaches its bound. Direct substitution and the corresponding geometric increments check the cases $n=0,1,2,3,4$; these are deductive checks, not computational experiments.

1. Main theorem
Theorem. For every nonnegative integer $n$, the maximum number of regions formed by $n$ full straight lines in the Euclidean plane is $M(n)=1+\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$. For positive $n$, the lines $y=ix+i^2$, with $i=1,\ldots,n$, attain this maximum; for $n=0$, the attaining arrangement is empty.
The proof has two independent tasks. First, an insertion argument bounds the count for arbitrary arrangements, without assuming any favorable intersection pattern. Second, the explicit family supplies exactly the distinct intersections required for equality at every insertion.

2. Lemma: regions are convex
Choose an affine equation for each existing line. On any connected component of the complement, each equation has a constant strict sign: a connected set avoiding its zero line cannot contain both positive and negative values. Thus every region lies in the set specified by one fixed choice of sides of all the lines.
Conversely, the set realizing any fixed collection of strict signs is an intersection of open half-planes. If nonempty, this set is open and convex, and therefore connected, so it lies in a single component of the complement. Together with the preceding containment, this proves that the regions are exactly the nonempty strict-sign sets.

3. Lemma: the exact increase from one new line
Let $L$ be a new line that does not coincide with any existing line, and let $m$ count its distinct intersection points with the existing lines. Removing those points leaves exactly $m+1$ connected pieces, including the unbounded pieces. When $m=0$, the sole piece is the whole line. Each piece lies in one old region because every old line equation has a constant nonzero sign along it.
Different pieces lie in different old regions. Indeed, if points from two pieces lay in the same convex region, the segment joining them would lie entirely in that region. That segment contains an old-line intersection point separating the pieces, which is impossible because a region avoids every old line.
Each region met by $L$ splits into exactly two regions. An intersection point inside the old region has a small disk contained in that region, so there are points on both sides of $L$. The intersections with the two open half-planes bounded by $L$ are nonempty and convex, and the sign of an equation for $L$ separates them into two components.
Regions not met by $L$ remain unchanged. Hence precisely $m+1$ old regions each become two, increasing the total by $m+1$. If the new line coincides with an existing line instead, the removed union is unchanged and the increase is zero.

4. Proof of the universal upper bound
Insert an arbitrary arrangement in any order, and let $r_k$ be the number of regions after the first $k$ lines. The empty arrangement gives $r_0=1$. If the $k$th line is not coincident with an earlier line, each of the preceding $k-1$ lines contributes at most one intersection point, so the number of distinct intersections is at most $k-1$.
The increment lemma therefore gives $r_k-r_{k-1}\leq k$. A coincident line also satisfies this inequality because it adds zero regions. Parallel lines contribute no intersection, and several earlier lines meeting the new line at one point contribute only one distinct intersection, so these degeneracies cannot invalidate the estimate.
Adding the incremental inequalities telescopes the region counts: $r_n\leq1+\sum_{k=1}^{n}k=1+\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$. The arithmetic-series sum gives the stated quadratic bound. Because the arrangement and its insertion order were arbitrary, this rules out every larger count.

5. Construction attaining the bound
For each positive integer $i$, define $L_i$ by $y=ix+i^2$. Distinct indices give distinct slopes, so these lines are neither parallel nor coincident. For any desired positive $n$, take the first $n$ members of this family.
Fix $k$ and an earlier index $i<k$. Equating the two line equations gives $(k-i)x=i^2-k^2=-(k-i)(i+k)$, and division by the nonzero number $k-i$ yields $x=-(i+k)$. Substitution then gives $y=-ik$, so the intersection point is $(-(i+k),-ik)$.
For fixed $k$, distinct earlier indices $i$ give distinct values of $-(i+k)$. Consequently, $L_k$ meets the earlier lines in exactly $k-1$ distinct points and adds exactly $k$ regions by the increment lemma. This includes $k=1$, when there are no earlier intersections and the first line adds one region.
Starting with one region, this construction has exactly $1+\sum_{k=1}^{n}k=1+\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$ regions. It therefore matches the universal upper bound for every positive $n$, proving both existence of a maximizing arrangement and the claimed maximum.

6. Empty arrangement and small cases
For $n=0$, no lines are removed and the whole plane is one region. The empty sum is zero, so the formula gives $M(0)=1$ without requiring a separate convention about positive-indexed lines.
For $n=0,1,2,3,4$, the formula gives $1,2,4,7,11$. Geometrically, these counts correspond to the empty plane, one dividing line, two intersecting lines, three pairwise-intersecting nonconcurrent lines, and a fourth line crossing the earlier three at distinct points. The explicit construction realizes the successive increases $1,2,3,4$; these checks illustrate the theorem rather than establish it.

C3 · established: Every region of a finite straight-line arrangement is open and convex; more precisely, it is a nonempty intersection of strict half-planes determined by the lines.
Each affine line equation has constant strict sign on a connected component of the complement. Conversely, any nonempty set realizing fixed strict signs is an intersection of open half-planes, hence open, convex, and connected. These two containments identify such sets with the regions.
C4 · established: A new line not coinciding with an earlier line increases the number of regions by exactly $m+1$, where $m$ is its number of distinct intersections with the earlier lines. A coincident line increases the count by zero.
The intersection points leave $m+1$ connected pieces on the new line. Each lies in an old region, and convexity prevents different pieces from lying in the same region. Openness ensures that each met region has points on both sides of the new line; its two half-plane intersections are nonempty and convex, so it splits into exactly two components. A coincident line leaves the union unchanged.
C5 · established: Every arrangement of $n$ lines has at most $1+n(n+1)/2$ regions.
The $k$th noncoincident line has at most $k-1$ distinct intersections with earlier lines and therefore adds at most $k$ regions. A repeated line adds zero. Starting from one region and summing gives $1+\sum_{k=1}^{n}k=1+n(n+1)/2$.
C6 · established: For every positive integer $n$, the lines $L_i: y=ix+i^2$, for $i=1,\ldots,n$, attain every incremental bound.
Their slopes are distinct. For $i<k$, the intersection of $L_i$ and $L_k$ is $(-(i+k),-ik)$. With $k$ fixed, these intersection points have distinct horizontal coordinates as $i$ varies, so $L_k$ has exactly $k-1$ distinct earlier intersections and adds exactly $k$ regions.
C7 · established: For every integer $n\geq0$, the maximum is $M(n)=1+n(n+1)/2$; the empty arrangement attains $M(0)=1$.
The explicit construction realizes the sum of all incremental upper bounds, so its count equals the universal bound. When $n=0$, the sum is empty and the complement is the whole connected plane.
C8 · established: For $n=0,1,2,3,4$, the maximum region counts are respectively $1,2,4,7,11$.
Substitution into the formula gives these values. The explicit arrangement realizes successive increases of $1,2,3,4$, providing geometric checks consistent with the general proof.

Conclusions
The maximum region count is $M(n)=1+n(n+1)/2$ for every nonnegative integer $n$. The upper bound follows because the $k$th line adds at most $k$ regions, and the family $y=ix+i^2$ realizes equality at every step.
The decisive geometric fact is the exact correspondence between pieces of an inserted line and old regions that it splits. Convexity prevents a region from being counted twice, while openness ensures that each encountered region genuinely divides into two. This supplies the bridge from intersection counting to the sharp extremal formula.
