Operation Narrow Passage: Conditional Options and the Limits of Assurance at Second Thomas Shoal

Abstract
Can an alliance protect personnel, preserve a threatened outpost, and avoid war when neither diplomacy nor military restraint guarantees control over escalation? This assessment examines a fictional crisis at Second Thomas Shoal through an actor-and-uncertainty map and a conditional analysis of operational feasibility. The evidence does not establish a viable strategy, but it identifies a decisive question: whether timely lifesaving assistance can be separated from permanent exclusion of the Philippine presence. A hypothetical adverse continuation illustrates why guaranteed success cannot be inferred from the brief; it does not establish that negotiated success is impossible. Holding temporarily, evacuating under an executable continuity arrangement, and confronting an explicit sacrifice among objectives remain conditional branches rather than validated recommendations. The assessment therefore distinguishes physical presence from legal claims, political assurances from operational compliance, and immediate de-escalation from durable protection against coercion. Its contribution is a bounded decision framework, not a certified solution.

Problem framing
The exercise places the Philippines, China, and the United States in a fictional crisis involving lethal violence, a demand for permanent Philippine departure, and a deteriorating occupied wreck. Its central operational problem is not simply whether to hold or withdraw. It is whether personnel protection, continued Philippine presence, independent alliance decision-making, and avoidance of wider conflict can remain compatible when the time available and the other parties' intentions are uncertain.
Success requires distinctions that the apparent hold-or-withdraw dilemma obscures. Physical continuity, preservation of legal claims, and alliance credibility are separate interests; a declaration reserving claims does not itself maintain an outpost. A substitute platform or temporary absence with an assured return might alter the choice, but neither Philippine acceptance nor practical implementation of such a substitute is established.
The assessment treats escalation control as a constraint rather than an available capability. The brief does not assume that any government wants war, but it also does not assume that political leaders can control every encounter or compel another sovereign government's participation. The appropriate question is therefore which conditional paths remain open and what evidence would justify selecting among them, not which response can be portrayed as risk-free.

Methods and evidence
The partial analysis mapped actors, stated objectives, decision authority, and information gaps against the fictional brief. It separated stipulated events from disputed attribution, unverified damage reports, and uncertain deadline consequences. This produced an analytical distinction between evidence of immediate danger and a reliable estimate of how long continued occupancy remains possible.
The analysis also constructed an adverse continuation to examine the sufficiency of a guaranteed-success claim. It considered an unsafe wreck, available evacuation, rejection of timely noncombat continuity arrangements, and armed conflict following an attempt to prevent Chinese possession by force. The continuation served as a logical possibility, not a forecast, probability estimate, or finding that diplomacy would fail.
A branching feasibility analysis identified conditions under which temporary holding or evacuation with continuity could remain candidates. The associated medical, engineering, diplomatic, and unit-compliance inquiry was proposed but not conducted. The available material does not document a completed comparative evaluation of feasible courses of action, external legal review, operational validation, or a completed research finding.

Mission, Evidence, and the Meaning of Success
Success at Second Thomas Shoal must be assessed as the joint protection of several interests, not simply as continued possession of the BRP Sierra Madre. The stipulated mission combines protection of Philippine and American personnel, prevention of the forcible elimination of the Philippine position, preservation of alliance credibility and independent decision-making, and avoidance of wider armed conflict with China. It also requires attention to Chinese personnel’s safety and the risk of further casualties. These are requirements against which to assess a response, not evidence that a response satisfying all of them exists.
The crisis is fictional: its confrontations, casualties, reports, and government decisions are invented, although the geographic setting, treaty relationships, and territorial dispute are real. Accordingly, the brief supplies the conditions of an exercise rather than an empirical record of operational performance. The available analysis is partial, with no completed, independently validated finding or established answer to the mission’s feasibility; it supplies neither an external legal assessment nor operational validation of proposed actions. Its appropriate use is to identify dependencies and consequential uncertainties, not to certify an executable solution.
Personnel safety and physical presence cannot be treated as interchangeable measures of success. A departure that protects those aboard could still fail the requirement to prevent coerced elimination of the Philippine position; continued occupation would not satisfy the full mission merely by retaining the wreck if personnel could not safely remain. The distinction matters because the brief makes protection of people and preservation of the position concurrent obligations. Treating either as sufficient would resolve the central tradeoff by redefining the mission rather than by meeting it.
Physical continuity, legal claims, and alliance credibility likewise require separate assessment. A declaration reserving Philippine claims does not itself maintain a physical position, while departure from an uninhabitable wreck would not by itself establish legal abandonment. A replacement platform or temporary absence with assured return might be considered a substitute for uninterrupted occupation, but the evidence does not establish Philippine acceptance of either as mission-equivalent. Nor does it establish that preservation of legal claims alone would preserve alliance credibility: accepting such substitutions requires an explicit political judgment, not an assumed equivalence.
Sovereign decision limits are constraints on the assessment rather than obstacles that a proposed strategy may assume away. Washington cannot dictate Philippine decisions, Manila cannot determine American military participation, and neither can compel Chinese cooperation. The exercise also withholds complete control over escalation: the absence of an intention to wage war does not establish that an encounter will remain bounded. A course of action must therefore be evaluated with its dependencies on other actors exposed, rather than described as successful on the assumption that those actors will make the required choices.
The analytical method begins with an actor-and-uncertainty map that distinguishes stated objectives, decision authority, constraints, and missing information. It separates conditions stipulated in the exercise from contested reports and analytical assumptions, then asks which unknowns could change the feasible action set. An adverse-continuation argument tests the stronger proposition of guaranteed success, understood as success across every continuation consistent with the brief. That test concerns the sufficiency of the information, not the likelihood of a particular outcome; a hypothetical failure case is not a forecast that negotiation must fail.
A branching feasibility framework then keeps candidate responses conditional on the circumstances that would make them available. It does not supply probabilities, agreed weights among competing interests, or a defensible ranking of options. The resulting standard is more demanding than either holding the wreck or ending the immediate confrontation: a response must demonstrate how it protects the distinct essential interests together, or identify the political choice by which one is sacrificed. This changes the assessment from a search for an assured maneuver to an examination of whether the mission’s requirements can actually be reconciled.

Competing Clocks and Decision Authority
The immediate operational constraint is not the announced deadline but whether personnel can remain alive and the outpost remain habitable long enough for an authorized response to take effect. The approximately six-hour evacuation deadline and the storm expected within approximately eighteen hours describe different pressures; neither establishes a safe period for deliberation. Continued holding therefore cannot be treated as an available option merely because the political deadline has not expired or the storm has not arrived.
Within the fiction, the confrontation has killed two Chinese coast guard personnel and three Philippine marines and wounded seven others. Fourteen Philippine military personnel remain aboard the Sierra Madre, including wounded marines, and one requires urgent surgery. These stipulated conditions establish an immediate personnel-safety problem, but they do not supply a reliable treatment window: the condition of several personnel cannot be independently verified. The assessment must consequently distinguish the established need for urgent care from any unsupported estimate of how long care can be deferred.
Structural danger creates a separate limit on continued occupancy. Engineering reports describe accelerated flooding, unreliable electrical systems, and potentially unsafe portions of the wreck, while the Philippine commander reports that abandoning certain compartments may be necessary to keep it afloat. Those reports indicate danger within the scenario, but the damage assessments have not been independently verified and provide no reliable estimate of remaining habitability. Moreover, withdrawing from exposed positions could ease Chinese boarding, making an action intended to sustain the wreck potentially costly to its defense. The relevant operational question is thus whether medically and structurally supportable occupancy remains possible, not simply how long Manila wishes to maintain it.
The Chinese deadline adds coercive pressure without resolving either physical limit. Beijing has announced evacuation by dawn but has not specified whether expiry will bring forcible boarding; American and Philippine officials do not know whether the announcement reflects an irrevocable leadership decision, a bargaining position, or instructions susceptible to different local interpretations. It would be unsupported to assume either that boarding is certain at expiry or that the period before expiry is protected from further confrontation. A diplomatic extension could alter this political clock without extending medical survivability or the wreck’s safe occupancy.
Decision authority is divided across actors whose public positions do not establish their minimum acceptable settlements. Manila has ordered continued occupation and requested immediate American assistance, while Beijing has offered evacuation, medical care, and safe passage only in exchange for permanent departure, excluding replacement personnel. Washington has affirmed its defense commitments but has not determined treaty applicability or an appropriate response. Manila retains authority over Philippine decisions but cannot determine American military participation; Washington cannot dictate Philippine withdrawal, and neither government can compel Chinese cooperation. These limits prevent an operational assessment from treating assistance, departure, or negotiated access as decisions available to a single alliance authority.
Contested attribution does not remove that division of authority or settle the legal questions. The videos provide conflicting apparent sequences, and their completeness, timestamps, and authenticity remain uncertain; both governments nevertheless publicly characterize the violence as an armed attack. Neither those descriptions nor uncertainty over the first shot establishes treaty applicability, legal authority, or the appropriate response. Treaty scope, consultation processes, proposed actions, and the provisional arrangement require assessment against their actual texts, which the supplied evidence does not resolve. Even definitive attribution would not itself make surgery accessible, repair the wreck, or establish a standstill.
Authority at the national level must also be distinguished from information and conduct at the scene. The Philippine commander has requested clarification of the order not to surrender if the wreck becomes uninhabitable, but no clarification has arrived; local knowledge of danger therefore coexists with unresolved instructions. Across the crisis, communications remain possible but have been delayed, contradicted, or qualified by different agencies. A political pause would not by itself establish compliance: confirmed receipt, accepted terms, and observed conduct by the relevant units are distinct requirements. Local interpretations and encounters could still affect outcomes, and no participant is granted control over every escalation.
Together, these constraints change the central question from how to use an assumed six-hour window to whether a timely response is physically feasible and can be authorized and implemented before an earlier safety limit intervenes. The decisive uncertainty is whether lifesaving assistance can be separated from permanent exclusion of the Philippine presence through a feasible noncombat arrangement. Until that relationship is established, neither an order to hold nor a political assurance demonstrates that personnel protection and preservation of the physical position can be achieved together.

Why the Brief Cannot Support a Guarantee
The brief cannot support a guarantee of mission success because it leaves open a continuation in which personnel safety, continued Philippine physical presence, and conflict avoidance cannot all be preserved. A guarantee here means success across every continuation consistent with the supplied information, not merely a reasonable prospect of success under favorable conditions. The appropriate test is therefore whether the brief excludes an incompatible-objectives contingency. The available analysis supports examining such a contingency, but does not constitute a completed operational or legal validation.
First, suppose that the Sierra Madre becomes unsafe before assistance can make continued occupation sustainable, while evacuation remains possible. This is an assumption for the test, not a finding about the wreck’s remaining endurance: the fictional brief supplies reports of deterioration but no reliable estimate of safe occupancy. Under this assumption, remaining aboard cannot provide both a sustainable position and protection of personnel. An order to hold does not remove the physical constraint, and promised assistance does not resolve it unless that assistance can arrive and sustain occupancy in time.
Next, suppose that Beijing rejects every timely, feasible noncombat arrangement that would separate lifesaving departure from permanent exclusion of the Philippine presence. This stipulation includes proposals for evacuation with assured return or for substitute Philippine presence; it does not assert that Beijing would actually reject them. The brief leaves Beijing’s minimum acceptable settlement unknown and does not establish an executable continuity arrangement. In this constructed continuation, negotiation fails to preserve physical continuity because rejection of every timely feasible arrangement is part of the assumption, not because diplomacy has been demonstrated to be ineffective.
Finally, suppose that Chinese units take possession after Philippine personnel depart, and that an attempt to prevent or reverse that possession by force produces armed conflict inconsistent with the mission’s conflict-avoidance requirement. These developments are not ruled out by the brief, but neither are they verified outcomes or assigned probabilities. The absence of a desire for war does not logically exclude refusal of concessions or a violent response to intervention. Nor can the assessment assume that an alliance decision to use force determines Beijing’s response or controls every subsequent escalation.
The incompatibility follows from the combination of these assumptions. Holding cannot preserve a safe, sustainable position; evacuation protects personnel but permits coerced loss of physical presence; and force used to prevent or reverse possession breaches the conflict-avoidance objective in this continuation. Negotiation supplies no additional successful route within the constructed case because every timely feasible noncombat continuity arrangement has been stipulated to fail. Failure to satisfy these core objectives jointly is enough to defeat a guarantee of the complete mission, without also determining the consequences for every other alliance interest.
That conclusion depends on retaining the distinction between physical presence and the interests that might survive its loss. A reservation of Philippine legal claims would not itself maintain personnel or an outpost at the shoal, while departure from an uninhabitable wreck would not by itself establish legal abandonment. Similarly, alliance credibility is a separate interest, not an established substitute for continued occupation. A replacement platform or temporary absence with assured return could change the feasibility assessment, but neither Philippine acceptance nor practical availability of such a substitute is established by the brief.
The adverse continuation is thus an analytical witness to insufficient information, not a prediction that every feasible negotiation will fail. Its force lies in showing that the supplied conditions do not warrant assurance across all admissible developments; it does not establish that a workable conditional strategy is impossible in the fictional crisis. If the assumed combination of unsafe occupancy and unavailable noncombat continuity were confirmed, a sovereign decision about which objective to sacrifice could become necessary. The central argument consequently shifts from seeking an unconditional promise of success to determining whether a timely, executable path can protect personnel without accepting permanent exclusion or requiring armed conflict.

Conditional Courses of Action and Their Dependencies
The available courses of action turn on whether lifesaving assistance can be separated from permanent exclusion of the Philippine presence, rather than on a choice between holding and withdrawing in the abstract. A branching feasibility assessment distinguishes temporary holding, evacuation or relocation with continuity, and a leadership decision about sacrificed objectives if neither noncombat path is available. The evidence does not contain a completed comparison of three feasible courses of action, and it supplies neither probabilities nor agreed tradeoff weights sufficient to rank the candidates. The branches therefore identify conditions for considering an action, not a preferred operational plan.
A time-bounded hold remains a candidate only while continued occupancy is medically and structurally supportable. Its immediate purpose would be to retain the physical position while seeking a verified pause, but continued presence cannot itself demonstrate that personnel can safely remain. Credible evidence that urgent treatment cannot be delivered while holding, or that the wreck cannot support continued occupancy, would remove the basis for this branch. Conversely, supportable occupancy would establish only that holding remains physically available, not that it is protected against boarding or further confrontation.
The pause sought alongside a hold has a separate operational dependency: political acceptance must translate into conduct by the relevant units. Delayed, inconsistent, or differently interpreted messages make an affirmative statement insufficient to establish that boarding has been suspended or access permitted. Confirmed receipt of instructions, accepted terms, and observed conduct are distinct requirements; silence from Beijing cannot substitute for any of them. A hold undertaken without such confirmation would remain exposed to uncertainty about local action even if national-level communications appeared encouraging.
Evacuation or relocation offers a different conditional route: protect personnel without accepting permanent exclusion, provided lifesaving access and an acceptable, executable continuity arrangement exist. Beijing’s stipulated offer does not meet that condition, because it links medical assistance and safe passage to permanent abandonment and excludes replacement personnel. A modified arrangement would therefore have to separate urgent assistance from that demand. Temporary absence with assured return or a substitute Philippine presence remains a candidate only if Manila accepts it as preserving its position and it can actually be implemented; neither is an established agreement.
Continuity in this branch is an operational requirement, not merely a diplomatic formulation. An assurance of return does not establish the transport, communications, access, or replacement-presence arrangements needed to make return possible. Nor would a reservation of legal claims by itself preserve the physical position that departure might leave exposed. The controlling question is whether an accepted arrangement can operate in time and whether the units able to obstruct or implement it will comply, not simply whether governments can announce compatible language.
If safe occupancy is unavailable and no timely, feasible noncombat continuity arrangement can be secured, the analysis reaches a decision about objectives rather than another technical maneuver. Leadership may then have to choose explicitly among personnel safety, preservation of physical presence, and avoidance of conflict. That condition must not be inferred merely from incomplete information or an unanswered diplomatic approach: uncertainty about a favorable branch is not evidence that every such branch has closed. Equally, an unverified assumption of safety or future cooperation cannot justify treating the favorable branch as available.
Military intervention cannot be inserted as a demonstrated solution to this residual tradeoff. The evidence establishes neither its availability nor its legal permissibility or practical effectiveness, and it provides no validated force, transport, medical, or replacement-presence plan on which to base that judgment. Failure to secure a noncombat arrangement would not supply those missing foundations or establish control over escalation. The operational implication is that preserving all essential interests depends on demonstrating a timely lifesaving-and-continuity path; where that path cannot be established, the unmet objective must remain visible to sovereign decision-makers rather than disappear inside an assumed military remedy.

Information That Could Change the Decision
A bounded information action could change the decision by testing whether timely lifesaving assistance can be separated from permanent exclusion of the Philippine presence. Its purpose would be to discriminate among feasible courses of action, not to implement a presumed diplomatic solution. The proposed action combines parallel medical and engineering assessment, authoritative inquiry into a short standstill and humanitarian access, clarification of national orders, and confirmation that relevant units acknowledge any agreed instructions. None of these components has been operationally validated in the supplied evidence.
The medical and engineering component should establish the earliest credible limit on safe occupancy and delayed treatment, including whether lifesaving care requires full evacuation. The scenario stipulates an urgent surgical need but supplies neither independently verified conditions for several other wounded personnel nor a reliable estimate of remaining habitability. Assessors would therefore need to distinguish evidence that some compartments are dangerous from evidence that the entire wreck must be evacuated, while identifying what remains uncertain. The announced boarding deadline and storm forecast cannot substitute for that assessment: neither establishes how long personnel can safely remain or how long treatment can wait.
The diplomatic component should ask an authoritative Chinese interlocutor whether Beijing would suspend boarding for a short, explicit period and permit urgent care without requiring permanent abandonment. Because the stated offer conditions assistance on departure without replacement, the inquiry would test whether that linkage is negotiable rather than assume that humanitarian language already provides a separate access route. It should also clarify what deadline expiry would mean, whether boarding is intended, and what temporary restrictions each party would accept. A response would be decision-relevant only insofar as its terms address the access and continuity problem; agreement merely to continue discussions would not establish either.
Manila would separately need to specify what it would accept as preservation of its position if the Sierra Madre becomes uninhabitable. An assurance of future return or a proposed substitute presence would require both Philippine acceptance and a practically executable means of implementation. The inquiry must therefore connect political terms to actual transport, medical, communications, and replacement-presence feasibility without assuming capabilities absent from the brief. Otherwise, an apparent agreement could leave the central question unanswered: whether departure for lifesaving reasons can occur without becoming permanent physical exclusion.
Any accepted standstill or access arrangement would also require evidence that the relevant units understand and act on it. Communications in the scenario have been delayed, contradicted, or qualified, and the interpretation of the deadline by local commanders remains uncertain. Reports should accordingly distinguish confirmed receipt of instructions, acknowledgment of their meaning, accepted terms, and observed conduct. These are different evidentiary states: a favorable political message would not establish local compliance, while acknowledgment alone would not demonstrate that access is occurring as agreed.
Manila’s clarification of its commander’s duties belongs within this action because medical and engineering findings do not themselves revise national orders. The commander has requested guidance on whether the prohibition against surrender still applies if the wreck becomes uninhabitable, and the scenario supplies no answer. Manila should explicitly address withdrawal from unsafe compartments and evacuation of an uninhabitable wreck rather than leave personnel to infer how potentially incompatible duties should be reconciled. This clarification would remain a Philippine decision; neither American assistance nor technical assessment would confer authority to make it on Manila’s behalf.
Legal review should proceed alongside these feasibility inquiries rather than be treated as resolved by public descriptions of the clash. Neither the disputed first shot nor either government’s use of “armed attack” settles treaty applicability, authority for a proposed action, or the appropriate response. The relevant treaty, consultation provisions, maritime rights, and provisional arrangement would need examination using their actual texts. Even definitive attribution would not, by itself, establish survivability, enable surgery, or secure operational observance of a standstill.
The information action must itself be assessed for delay and signaling risks. Requests for access and diplomatic approaches may alter the parties’ behavior, and the evidence does not establish that useful answers can arrive before urgent decisions become unavoidable. Diplomatic contact should therefore not be made a prerequisite for urgent medically indicated care, although this principle does not establish that safe access is available. An unanswered inquiry must remain an unresolved condition, not be counted as either permission to proceed or proof that every noncombat arrangement has been rejected.
The discriminating outcome would be independently supported evidence that personnel can survive long enough for assistance, that care or evacuation is physically accessible, and that an accepted continuity arrangement can be implemented by the units concerned. Conversely, an imminent safety limit combined with refusal of every timely feasible noncombat continuity arrangement would support the conclusion that the objectives cannot all be preserved through that path. The evidence thus makes demonstrated access, survivability, and continuity—not reassurance or attribution alone—the basis for treating a noncombat option as genuinely available.

Durable Interests and the Unresolved Decision
Immediate de-escalation would protect only part of the alliance’s mission; the unresolved test is whether an arrangement also protects against renewed coercion without making continued personnel exposure the price of credibility. In this fictional crisis, stopping the confrontation would not by itself demonstrate that deadly coercion had failed, that another confrontation had become less likely, or that alliance decision-making remained independent. These are distinct criteria for judging a settlement, not effects that can be inferred from an absence of further gunfire.
The distinction matters particularly if lifesaving assistance becomes available through a temporary accommodation. Such an accommodation could address urgent danger while leaving unresolved whether Philippine presence can continue on acceptable terms. Conversely, maintaining personnel aboard would not establish durable protection if safe occupancy and subsequent support remained uncertain. The relevant assessment must therefore ask both whether a settlement avoids converting violence into permanent exclusion and whether it offers an alternative to repeatedly exposing personnel to an unresolved physical and political danger; the supplied evidence establishes neither longer-term effect.
Alliance credibility likewise cannot be reduced to either immediate military intervention or immediate restraint. The scenario identifies possible credibility costs if Washington stands aside during a forcible loss of the outpost, but also the danger that pressure for Philippine withdrawal could appear to substitute American judgment for a sovereign ally’s decision. Preserving independence consequently remains a constraint on the response: Manila cannot determine American military participation, and Washington cannot determine which losses Manila must accept. Agreement between the allies would still not establish that Beijing would cooperate or that a settlement would deter recurrence.
The evidence supplies no defensible overall ranking of the candidate courses of action. It provides neither probabilities for the relevant continuations nor agreed weights for comparing personnel safety, physical presence, alliance credibility, and conflict avoidance. A branch that becomes operationally feasible is not thereby preferable across all these interests, and a branch that reduces immediate danger is not thereby a durable settlement. The available analysis is partial rather than a completed, independently validated finding, and no cited external sources establish the treaty interpretations, maritime rights, or operational capabilities needed to support a stronger recommendation.
If safe occupancy proves unavailable and every timely, feasible noncombat arrangement preserving an acceptable Philippine presence is rejected, an explicit sovereign choice about sacrificed objectives would become necessary. That is a conditional decision requirement, not a finding that this point has already been reached. Leadership would need to identify which objective it was relinquishing or placing at risk, rather than describe evacuation as preserving physical presence, continued exposure as assured personnel protection, or force as compatible with assured conflict avoidance. Operational analysis can clarify those incompatibilities; it cannot resolve them by silently changing the mission’s priorities.
The central argument therefore turns on a narrower distinction than success or failure in ending the immediate confrontation. A viable strategy would require an executable separation of lifesaving assistance from permanent exclusion, followed by a defensible assessment of what the resulting arrangement preserves over time. The supplied evidence leaves that possibility open without establishing it, while also leaving open a contingency in which the objectives conflict. What changes is the burden on the decision: assurance cannot substitute for demonstrated feasibility, and, if feasible continuity is exhausted, an acknowledged political sacrifice cannot be presented as a technical solution that protects every essential interest.

The record contains partial reasoning but reports no completed research finding and incomplete verification. The available argument is not an independently validated operational assessment.
The bounded evidence selection does not establish exhaustive review of saved material. Its source-level references are coarse, and omitted notes cannot be assumed to confirm the outline.
No treaty texts, maritime-law analysis, validated transport or medical plan, or executable replacement-presence arrangement are supplied. Legal and operational feasibility therefore remain unresolved.

The adverse continuation depends on hypothetical rejection of all timely feasible continuity arrangements. It illustrates the limits of assurance but does not establish the probability of failure or the impossibility of a viable strategy.

Conclusions
The evidence does not establish a viable strategy for achieving the full mission, but it also does not establish that such a strategy is impossible. The adverse continuation supports an information-limited objection to a guarantee, not a forecast that diplomacy or every conditional response will fail.
The most consequential feasibility question is whether timely lifesaving assistance can be separated from permanent exclusion of the Philippine physical position. Temporary holding and evacuation with continuity remain candidates only if their medical, structural, political, and implementation conditions are established.
A proposed parallel inquiry could help discriminate among these branches, but its timeliness, safety, and effectiveness are untested. If no safe noncombat continuity path remains, leadership must explicitly choose among objectives rather than conceal the tradeoff in a technical recommendation.
Immediate de-escalation would not by itself demonstrate durable protection against coercion or preservation of alliance credibility. Those outcomes require further assessment, and no completed legal or operational validation is available.
