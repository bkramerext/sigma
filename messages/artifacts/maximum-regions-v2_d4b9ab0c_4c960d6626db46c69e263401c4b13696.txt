The Sharp Region Bound for Straight Lines: An Increment Lemma and an Explicit Construction

Abstract
How many connected regions can finitely many straight lines create in the Euclidean plane? For every nonnegative integer $n$, the maximum is $M(n)=1+n(n+1)/2$. The argument separates the obstruction to larger arrangements from the existence of a maximizing one. Regions are open convex intersections of half-planes, so a new line meeting the previous lines at $m$ distinct points creates exactly $m+1$ additional regions. Consequently, the $k$th line adds at most $k$ regions. The explicit family $y=ix+i^2$ attains this bound at every insertion, because its intersections with earlier lines are distinct along the new line. The empty arrangement and small cases are included. This gives a self-contained deductive argument that covers arbitrary placements, including parallel, concurrent, and repeated lines, without relying on numerical extrapolation.

Problem framing
Let $n$ be a nonnegative integer, and consider $n$ full straight lines in the Euclidean plane. A region is a connected component of the complement of their union. Write $M(n)$ for the greatest region count attainable by such an arrangement; parallel, concurrent, and repeated lines are allowed.
The extremal question has two separate requirements: a bound valid for every arrangement and an arrangement attaining that bound for each $n$. Small configurations provide useful checks, but they cannot establish either requirement for all $n$.

Methods and evidence
The analysis counted regions by inserting lines one at a time. It first identified regions with nonempty intersections of open half-planes, then used convexity to relate the pieces of an inserted line to the old regions that it splits. This yielded an exact increment formula before any maximizing arrangement was specified.
Attainment was analyzed separately through the family $L_i: y=ix+i^2$. Solving its pairwise intersection equations established the distinct-intersection property needed for equality at each insertion. The resulting count was compared with the universal bound, and the cases $n=0,1,2,3,4$ were checked deductively; no computational experiment was performed.

1. Main result
Theorem. For every nonnegative integer $n$, the maximum number of regions formed by $n$ full straight lines in the Euclidean plane is
$M(n)=1+\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$.
For $n\geq 1$, an attaining arrangement consists of the lines $L_i: y=ix+i^2$ for $i=1,\ldots,n$; for $n=0$, it is the empty arrangement.
The proof first bounds the increase caused by an arbitrary inserted line. It then verifies that the displayed family realizes the largest permitted increase at every step, thereby proving both the upper bound and attainment.

2. Lemma: regions are open and convex
Choose an affine equation for each existing line. On a connected component of their complement, each affine function has a constant strict sign: a continuous function cannot take both positive and negative values on a connected set without taking zero. Thus every region lies in the set specified by one fixed choice of sides of all the lines.
Conversely, the set realizing any fixed choice of strict signs is an intersection of open half-planes. If nonempty, this set is open and convex, hence connected, and lies in the complement of the lines. It must therefore lie in one connected component; together with the preceding containment, this proves that regions are exactly the nonempty sign cells.
With no lines, nothing is removed from the plane. The complement is the entire plane, which is connected, so the initial region count is one.

3. Lemma: the exact increment from one line
Let $L$ be a new line that does not coincide with an existing line, and suppose it meets the old lines at $m$ distinct points. Removing those points leaves exactly $m+1$ connected pieces of $L$, including the unbounded pieces. Every piece lies in one old region because all old affine equations have constant nonzero signs along it.
Different pieces lie in different old regions. Indeed, if two pieces met the same old region, convexity would put the segment joining a point of one piece to a point of the other entirely inside that region. The segment contains an old-line intersection point separating the pieces, which cannot belong to any old region.
Each region met by $L$ is split into exactly two regions. At a point of the intersection, openness provides a small disk contained in the old region, so the region has points on both sides of $L$. Its intersections with the two open half-planes bounded by $L$ are therefore nonempty and convex; they are connected and separated from each other in the complement of $L$.
There are consequently $m+1$ old regions that are each replaced by two, while all other old regions remain unchanged. The net increase is exactly $m+1$. If $L$ instead coincides with an old line, the union of lines does not change, and the increase is zero.

4. Proof of the universal upper bound
Insert an arbitrary arrangement in any order, and let $R_k$ be the number of regions after the first $k$ insertions. A $k$th line that is not coincident with an earlier line meets each of the preceding $k-1$ lines at most once, so it has at most $k-1$ distinct old-line intersection points. The increment lemma gives $R_k-R_{k-1}\leq k$; a coincident insertion also satisfies this inequality because its increase is zero.
Starting from $R_0=1$ and adding the increment bounds gives
$R_n\leq 1+\sum_{k=1}^{n}k=1+\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$.
The sum is the arithmetic progression from $1$ to $n$, with average $(n+1)/2$. Parallel lines contribute no intersection with the new line, and concurrence merges intersection points, so neither can invalidate the bound.

5. An explicit arrangement attaining the bound
For each positive integer $i$, take $L_i: y=ix+i^2$. These lines have distinct slopes, so no pair is parallel or coincident. To count the increment when inserting $L_k$, solve its intersection with $L_i$ for $i<k$.
The equation $ix+i^2=kx+k^2$ gives
$x=-(i+k),\qquad y=-ik$.
For fixed $k$, different earlier indices $i$ give different values of $-(i+k)$. Thus $L_k$ meets the preceding $k-1$ lines at exactly $k-1$ distinct points, not merely at that many intersections counted with multiplicity.
The increment lemma now shows that $L_k$ creates exactly $k$ new regions. This includes $k=1$: there are no old intersection points, the new line has one piece, and it splits the initial region into two. Taking the first $n$ members of the family therefore gives exactly $1+\sum_{k=1}^{n}k$ regions for every positive $n$.
This construction reaches the universal upper bound, so the bound is the maximum rather than only an estimate. At $n=0$, the family is empty and the sum has no terms, giving one region as required. This completes the deductive proof of the stated formula.

6. Boundary cases and small-value checks
For $n=0,1,2,3,4$, the formula yields $M(n)=1,2,4,7,11$, respectively. Geometrically, these counts correspond to the empty plane, one dividing line, two intersecting lines, three pairwise-intersecting nonconcurrent lines, and a fourth line meeting the first three at distinct points.
In the explicit construction, the successive increases are $1,2,3,4$. These checks agree with the general argument, but the proof does not extrapolate from them: it rests on the increment lemma and the intersection calculation valid for every insertion.

Conclusions
The maximum is $M(n)=1+n(n+1)/2$ for every nonnegative integer $n$. The universal insertion bound rules out larger arrangements, and the explicit family $y=ix+i^2$ attains every allowed increment.
The essential geometric bridge is that arrangement regions are open and convex. This makes the pieces cut out on an inserted line correspond to distinct regions, each split exactly once, and turns a local intersection count into the sharp global bound.
