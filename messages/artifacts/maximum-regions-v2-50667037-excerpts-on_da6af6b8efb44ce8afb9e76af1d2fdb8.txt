The Maximum Number of Planar Regions Cut by Straight Lines: A Sharp Incremental Bound

Abstract
How many regions can a finite collection of straight lines create in the Euclidean plane? For every nonnegative integer $n$, the sharp maximum is $M(n)=1+n(n+1)/2$. The argument separates a universal upper bound from an explicit construction attaining it. Its central observation is that a new line meeting the existing arrangement at $m$ distinct points creates exactly $m+1$ new regions, unless it coincides with an existing line. Convexity of the existing regions justifies this exact count. Consequently, the $k$th line adds at most $k$ regions. The family $y=ix+i^2$ attains every incremental bound because its intersections with earlier lines are distinct. The empty arrangement and small cases complete the boundary checks. This gives a self-contained deductive explanation of both the bound and its attainability.

Problem framing
Let $n$ be a nonnegative integer, and consider $n$ full straight lines in the Euclidean plane. A region is a connected component of the complement of their union; both bounded and unbounded components count. Write $M(n)$ for the maximum possible number of regions. The upper-bound argument allows parallel lines, several lines through one point, and repeated lines.
The extremal question has two distinct requirements: every arrangement must satisfy the proposed bound, and an admissible arrangement must attain it for every $n$. Small examples alone establish neither requirement. The argument below addresses them separately and includes the empty arrangement.

Methods and evidence
The analysis counted regions incrementally. It first identified the regions as nonempty intersections of open half-planes, then used their convexity to determine exactly how many regions an inserted line splits. Bounding the number of distinct intersections on that line yielded a universal upper bound.
Attainability was checked algebraically for the explicit family $L_i: y=ix+i^2$. The intersection coordinates established that each insertion achieves its upper bound. Direct substitution and the corresponding geometric increments checked the cases $n=0,1,2,3,4$; these checks were not used as a proof of the general formula.

1. Main result
Theorem. For every nonnegative integer $n$, the maximum number of regions formed by $n$ full straight lines in the Euclidean plane is
$M(n)=1+\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$.
For positive $n$, the lines $L_i: y=ix+i^2$, with $i=1,\ldots,n$, attain this maximum. For $n=0$, the empty arrangement attains it.
The proof rests on an exact insertion count. Establishing that count requires more than counting pieces on the new line: one must show that different pieces split different existing regions, and that each split creates exactly one additional region.

2. Lemma: regions are open and convex
Choose an affine equation for each existing line. On a connected component of the complement, each affine expression has constant strict sign: a continuous expression cannot take both positive and negative values on a connected set without taking zero. Thus every region lies in the set specified by one fixed choice of sides of all the lines.
Conversely, the set realizing any fixed choice of strict signs is an intersection of open half-planes. If nonempty, it is open and convex, hence connected, and it avoids every line. It must therefore lie in one connected component; together with the preceding containment, this identifies it with that component. With no lines, the complement is the whole plane and has one region.

3. Lemma: the exact increase from one new line
Let $L$ be a new line that does not coincide with any existing line, and let $m$ be the number of distinct points where it meets the existing arrangement. Removing those points leaves exactly $m+1$ connected pieces, including unbounded pieces; when $m=0$, the sole piece is the whole line. Each piece lies in one old region because all existing affine expressions have constant nonzero signs along it.
Different pieces lie in different old regions. Otherwise, points chosen from two pieces would belong to one convex region, so the segment joining them would remain in that region. That segment lies on $L$ and contains an intersection with an old line, contradicting the definition of a region.
Every old region met by $L$ splits into exactly two regions. At a point of intersection with that open region, a small disk lies inside the region and contains points on both sides of $L$. The intersections of the region with the two open half-planes bounded by $L$ are therefore nonempty and convex, so each is connected; the sign of an equation for $L$ separates them. Regions not met by $L$ remain unchanged.
There are consequently $m+1$ affected old regions, and replacing each by two increases the total by exactly $m+1$. If the inserted line instead coincides with an old line, the removed union does not change and the increase is zero.

4. Universal upper bound
Insert the lines of an arbitrary arrangement in any order, and let $R_k$ be the region count after the first $k$ insertions. A noncoincident $k$th line meets each of the preceding $k-1$ lines in at most one point. It therefore has at most $k-1$ distinct intersection points, and the insertion lemma gives $R_k-R_{k-1}\leq k$. A repeated line adds zero and satisfies the same inequality.
Starting from $R_0=1$, summing the incremental inequalities gives
$R_n=1+\sum_{k=1}^{n}(R_k-R_{k-1})\leq 1+\sum_{k=1}^{n}k=1+\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$.
Parallel lines and several lines passing through one point do not invalidate this bound: they reduce, rather than increase, the number of distinct intersections available on an inserted line.

5. A construction attaining the bound
For each positive integer $i$, take $L_i: y=ix+i^2$. Distinct slopes ensure that no two of these lines are parallel or coincident. For $i<k$, their intersection satisfies
$ix+i^2=kx+k^2,\qquad x=-(i+k),\qquad y=-ik$.
For fixed $k$, the coordinates $-(i+k)$ are distinct as $i$ ranges from $1$ to $k-1$. Hence $L_k$ has exactly $k-1$ distinct intersections with the earlier lines. The insertion lemma gives an increase of exactly $k$ regions, including $k=1$, when the first line adds one region.
Taking the first $n$ members of this family therefore produces exactly
$1+\sum_{k=1}^{n}k=1+\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$
regions. This matches the universal upper bound and proves the stated maximum for every positive $n$. For $n=0$, the sum is empty and the plane itself is the single region, so the same formula holds.

6. Boundary checks
For $n=0,1,2,3,4$, the formula gives $1,2,4,7,11$ regions. Geometrically, these correspond to the empty plane, one dividing line, two intersecting lines, three pairwise-intersecting nonconcurrent lines, and a fourth line crossing the first three at distinct points. The successive positive increments are $1,2,3,4$, as guaranteed by the construction; these examples check the formula without replacing its general proof.

Reasoning step 2 shows that the $m+1$ pieces of the inserted line occupy distinct old regions by convexity. Openness supplies points on both sides of the line in each met region, and the two half-plane intersections are precisely its two new connected components.
C5 · established: Every arrangement of $n$ lines has at most $1+n(n+1)/2$ regions.
Reasoning step 3 bounds the distinct intersections on a noncoincident $k$th line by $k-1$, hence its region increment by $k$. Coincident lines add zero. Summing from the initial single region gives the stated bound.
C6 · established: For $L_i: y=ix+i^2$, the intersection of $L_i$ and $L_k$ with $i<k$ is $(-(i+k),-ik)$. Thus $L_k$ meets the preceding lines at exactly $k-1$ distinct points and adds exactly $k$ regions.
Reasoning step 4 solves the pair of line equations. The slopes are distinct, and for fixed $k$ the intersection abscissae $-(i+k)$ are distinct as $i$ varies, so the exact increment lemma applies with $m=k-1$.

Limitations
The result concerns full straight lines in the entire Euclidean plane, with all connected complementary regions counted. It does not address segments, bounded viewing windows, or other ambient spaces.
The proof is presented as a self-contained deductive argument, not as independently or formally verified mathematics. The supplied evidence has inconsistent completion labels, but its detailed reasoning identifies no remaining mathematical gap; those labels do not themselves provide a counterexample or a missing proof step.

Conclusions
The sharp maximum is $M(n)=1+n(n+1)/2$ for every nonnegative integer $n$. The upper bound holds for arbitrary arrangements, while the explicit family $y=ix+i^2$ attains it for every positive $n$ and the empty arrangement handles $n=0$.
The decisive bridge is the exact insertion lemma: distinct intersection points partition a new line into pieces that split distinct convex regions. This converts a geometric extremal problem into a sum of sharp incremental bounds.
