A Sharp Region Bound for Straight Lines in the Plane

Abstract
How many regions can a finite collection of straight lines create in the Euclidean plane? For every nonnegative integer $n$, the maximum is $M(n)=1+n(n+1)/2$. The argument separates a universal upper bound from an explicit construction attaining it. Its key observation is that a new line meeting the existing arrangement at $m$ distinct points creates exactly $m+1$ additional regions, provided it does not coincide with an existing line. Convexity of the old regions justifies this exact count and prevents overcounting. Consequently, the $k$th line adds at most $k$ regions. The lines $y=ix+i^2$, for $i=1,\ldots,n$, attain every incremental bound because their intersections are distinct along each newly inserted line. The empty arrangement and small cases agree with the formula. This gives a deductive explanation of both the bound and its attainability, rather than an extrapolation from examples.

Problem framing
Let $n$ be a nonnegative integer. An arrangement consists of $n$ full straight lines in the Euclidean plane, and a region is a connected component of the complement of their union. Write $M(n)$ for the largest region count attainable by such an arrangement. The upper-bound argument permits parallel, concurrent, and repeated lines, so it does not assume that the arrangement has a favorable intersection pattern.
The extremal question has two distinct requirements: no arrangement may exceed the proposed count, and some arrangement must attain it for every $n$. Small examples alone cannot establish either requirement in general. The argument below relates regions to the pieces of an inserted line, then constructs a family for which each insertion contributes as many new regions as the universal bound allows.

Methods and evidence
The analysis proceeded by exact incremental counting. It first identified regions as nonempty intersections of open half-planes, then used their convexity to determine the change caused by inserting one line. Summing the resulting bounds gave a universal estimate, while an algebraic intersection calculation for a separate explicit family established attainment. Direct substitution and geometric interpretation checked the cases $n=0,1,2,3,4$; these checks were not used to infer the general result.

1. Main result
Theorem. For every nonnegative integer $n$, the maximum number of regions formed by $n$ full straight lines in the Euclidean plane is
$M(n)=1+\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$.
For positive $n$, an attaining arrangement is given by $L_i: y=ix+i^2$ for $i=1,\ldots,n$. For $n=0$, the attaining arrangement is empty.
The proof has two separate parts. First, an arbitrary insertion order yields an upper bound on the number of new regions created by each line. Second, the displayed family realizes equality at every insertion, proving that the upper bound is an actual maximum.

2. Lemma: regions are open and convex
Choose an affine equation for each line, with the line as its zero set. On a connected component of the complement, each equation has a constant strict sign: a continuous function cannot take both positive and negative values on a connected set while avoiding zero. Thus every region lies in the set specified by one fixed collection of signs.
Conversely, the set realizing any fixed collection of strict signs is an intersection of open half-planes. It is open and convex, and, when nonempty, connected. It therefore lies in a single connected component of the complement; together with the preceding containment, this identifies it with that component. Hence every region is open and convex.

3. Lemma: the exact contribution of a new line
Let $L$ be a new line that does not coincide with any existing line, and let $m$ be the number of distinct points where it meets the old arrangement. Removing these points leaves exactly $m+1$ connected pieces of $L$, including its unbounded pieces. When $m=0$, the sole piece is the whole line. Each piece lies in one old region because it is connected and avoids every old line.
Different pieces lie in different old regions. Otherwise, choose a point from each of two pieces in the same region. Convexity would place the segment joining them inside that region, but the segment contains an intersection with an old line. Such an intersection cannot belong to a region of the old complement.
Now let $U$ be an old region met by $L$. A meeting point has a small disk contained in $U$, since $U$ is open, so $U$ contains points on both sides of $L$. The intersections of $U$ with the two open half-planes bounded by $L$ are nonempty and convex, hence connected; the separating line prevents them from belonging to one component after its removal. Thus $U$ is replaced by exactly two regions.
There are exactly $m+1$ old regions met by $L$, one for each piece, and all other old regions remain unchanged. Each affected region contributes a net increase of one, so the total increase is exactly $m+1$. If the new line instead coincides with an existing line, the union of the lines does not change, and the increase is zero.

4. The universal upper bound
Consider any arrangement and insert its lines in any order. Write $R_k$ for the region count after the first $k$ insertions. A noncoincident $k$th line meets each of the preceding $k-1$ lines at at most one point, so the number of distinct intersection points on it is at most $k-1$. The exact contribution lemma therefore gives $R_k-R_{k-1}\leq k$; a coincident insertion also satisfies this inequality because it contributes zero.
With no lines, the entire plane is one region, so $R_0=1$. Adding the successive inequalities counts the total increase from the empty arrangement:
$R_n=1+\sum_{k=1}^{n}(R_k-R_{k-1})\leq 1+\sum_{k=1}^{n}k=1+\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$.
Parallel lines contribute no intersection with one another, and several old lines meeting the new line at the same point contribute only one distinct point. Neither degeneracy can increase the bound.

5. An explicit arrangement attaining the bound
For each positive integer $i$, take the line $L_i: y=ix+i^2$. Its slope is $i$, so the lines are pairwise nonparallel and distinct. For $i<k$, their intersection satisfies
$ix+i^2=kx+k^2$.
Rearranging gives $(k-i)x=i^2-k^2=-(k-i)(i+k)$; since $k-i\neq 0$, the intersection coordinates are $x=-(i+k)$ and $y=-ik$.
Fix $k$. As $i$ ranges from $1$ to $k-1$, the values $-(i+k)$ are all distinct. Thus $L_k$ meets the preceding lines at exactly $k-1$ distinct points, rather than merely intersecting every preceding line with possible coincidences among those intersections. The exact contribution lemma shows that inserting $L_k$ creates exactly $k$ new regions.
For $k=1$, there are no old intersection points, and the first line creates exactly one additional region, as required. Taking the first $n$ lines therefore gives
$R_n=1+\sum_{k=1}^{n}k=1+\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$.
This count matches the universal upper bound, proving the theorem for every positive integer $n$.

6. The empty arrangement and small cases
For $n=0$, no line is removed, and the whole plane is connected. The sum in the counting argument is empty and equals zero, so $M(0)=1$. This establishes the boundary case without requiring a positive-indexed construction.
For $n=0,1,2,3,4$, the formula gives $1,2,4,7,11$ regions, respectively. Geometrically, these correspond to the empty plane, one dividing line, two intersecting lines, three pairwise-intersecting nonconcurrent lines, and a fourth line crossing the preceding three at distinct points. The explicit construction realizes the successive increases $1,2,3,4$. These cases illustrate the proved counting mechanism rather than supplying an induction from observed values.

Conversely, the set realizing a fixed collection of strict signs is an intersection of open half-planes; if nonempty, it is convex and connected. These containments identify the sign cells with the regions.
C3 · established: A new line that does not coincide with an old line and has $m$ distinct intersection points with the old arrangement increases the region count by exactly $m+1$. A coincident line increases the count by zero.
The intersection points cut the new line into $m+1$ pieces. Convexity ensures that distinct pieces belong to distinct old regions. Openness ensures that each region met by the line has points on both sides, and convexity makes each side connected, so every such region is replaced by exactly two.
C4 · established: Every arrangement of $n$ lines has at most $1+\sum_{k=1}^{n}k=1+n(n+1)/2$ regions.
A noncoincident $k$th line meets the preceding $k-1$ lines at at most $k-1$ distinct points, so the exact increment lemma bounds its contribution by $k$. A coincident line contributes zero. Summing from the initial single region gives the bound.
C5 · established: For every positive integer $n$, the lines $L_i: y=ix+i^2$, for $i=1,\ldots,n$, add exactly $k$ regions when $L_k$ is inserted.
Distinct slopes exclude parallel and coincident pairs. For $i<k$, the intersection of $L_i$ and $L_k$ is $(-(i+k),-ik)$. With $k$ fixed, these points have distinct horizontal coordinates as $i$ varies, giving exactly $k-1$ distinct intersections and hence exactly $k$ new regions.

Limitations
The result concerns full straight lines and connected components in the Euclidean plane; it does not assert a corresponding formula for segments, curves, or a bounded viewing window. The supplied argument is deductive but has not been independently or formally verified. Conclusions
The sharp bound is $M(n)=1+n(n+1)/2$ for every nonnegative integer $n$. Its essential geometric ingredient is the exact insertion count: a noncoincident line with $m$ distinct old intersection points creates $m+1$ new regions. The family $y=ix+i^2$ attains the largest permitted increment at every step, completing the matching upper-bound and construction argument.
